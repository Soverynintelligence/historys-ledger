The Founding 1763–1789

Before you read the record, say where you stand. You'll be asked again at the end, and shown whether you moved.

The question

A government was founded on the written principle that all men are created equal, by men who held people in bondage and wrote protections for that bondage into the structure of the state. How should the two be weighed?

Choose one to continue
IEstablished facts

What the evidence strongly supports.

What was achieved

  • A written claim that authority derives from the consent of the governed, adopted 4 July 1776.
  • A government structured to limit itself, ratified 1788.
  • Power handed from one faction to a rival without a sword, 1801.

What it cost

  • Slavery was protected in the structure of the state: representation counted enslaved people at three-fifths; the slave trade was shielded from prohibition until 1808; escaped people were to be returned.
  • The 1790 census counted 697,681 enslaved people. Estimates for 1776 run nearer 450,000–500,000.
  • Jefferson, who wrote the words, held people in bondage while writing them.

Both columns are drawn from the same documents. Neither is our characterisation.

IIPrimary sources

The words, laws and letters from the time.

“We hold these truths to be self-evident, that all men are created equal…”
Declaration of Independence, 4 July 1776
Every government on earth in 1776 justified itself by blood, conquest, or God's appointment of a king. The men in Philadelphia wrote down a different reason.
“He has waged cruel war against human nature itself, violating its most sacred rights of life & liberty in the persons of a distant people who never offended him…”
Jefferson's draft of the Declaration — struck by Congress before signing
Jefferson later recorded why it came out: South Carolina and Georgia objected, and northern merchants who had carried the trade were sensitive on the point.
“…they have in common with all other men a natural and unalienable right to that freedom which the great Parent of the Universe hath bestowed equally on all mankind…”
Petition of enslaved men to the Massachusetts legislature, 1777
The contradiction was named at the time, out loud, by the people it excluded — using the founders' own language, within a year of the Declaration.
IIIConflicting accounts

Where the record disagrees with itself.

“How is it that we hear the loudest yelps for liberty among the drivers of negroes?”
Samuel Johnson, Taxation No Tyranny, 1775
Written by a critic of the American cause, from within the empire the colonists were leaving — the charge of hypocrisy is contemporary, not retrospective.
“I tremble for my country when I reflect that God is just…”
Jefferson, Notes on the State of Virginia, 1785
The same man who did not free the people he held wrote down that the arrangement was indefensible. Both facts are in the record; neither cancels the other.

The record does not speak with one voice. This is the part a textbook usually removes.

IVWhat remains unknown

The limits of the record, said plainly.

  • No full record survives of the debate in which the slave-trade passage was struck. Jefferson's account is the main source, written decades later, by an interested party.
  • Enslaved population figures before the 1790 census are estimates, not counts. Ranges differ by tens of thousands.
  • What the delegates privately believed cannot be recovered. Their votes and their words survive; their minds do not.

Where the record stops, we stop. That is the fourth column of every entry.
