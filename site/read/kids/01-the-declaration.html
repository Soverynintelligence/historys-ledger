What was achieved

They wrote, in public, that a government’s power comes from the people — and that people have rights no king gave them. That claim is in the Declaration, dated July 4, 1776.

What it cost

They did not include everyone in that “people.” Enslaved people, women, and Native nations were left out, and the people left out said so in writing at the time.

Both columns are drawn from the same documents. Neither is our characterisation.

They put it on paper

In 1776, most countries had a king. Kings said they were in charge because of blood, or because God picked them.

The people who broke away from Britain wrote a different reason, out loud, where anyone could read it: a government only gets its power if the people agree. If it stops protecting their rights, the people can change it.

They did not whisper that. They signed it and sent it into the world. That paper is the Declaration of Independence. The date on it is July 4, 1776.

Self-evident means they thought you should not need a king to tell you this. Equal here means born with the same rights — not that every person is the same height or the same rich.

The next part of the same sentence names three of those rights: life, liberty, and the pursuit of happiness. And it says governments are built to protect those rights, with power that comes from the people.

Why they were angry

For years Britain had been taxing the colonies. The fight was not only that the taxes were high. It was that they had no vote in the Parliament that taxed them.

A writer named Thomas Paine said the whole setup was backwards, in words ordinary people could read:

He meant: a huge place should not be run by a small island far away. That pamphlet spread fast. Then came the Declaration.

The people left out said so right away

Here is the part many tellings skip. Nobody had to wait a hundred years to notice the problem. The people who were left out used the founders’ own sentence against them — at the time.

When they wrote “” hundreds of thousands of people in the new country were enslaved. Women could not vote. Native nations were treated as being in the way.

Abigail Adams wrote to her husband John while he was helping build the new laws:

She was asking him to remember women. His answer was to laugh.

Enslaved people quoted the Declaration too. In Massachusetts, a woman named Elizabeth Freeman heard a constitution read aloud that said all men are born free and equal. She walked to a lawyer, sued, and won her freedom in court.

The words were already doing work the writers had not fully meant. That is why the sentence mattered: other people could pick it up.

What the Declaration is — and is not

The Declaration is a claim. It says what they believed a government is for. It is not yet the rulebook for how the country would run day to day. That rulebook comes next: the Constitution, in 1787.

So you can hold two true things at once:

  • They wrote a promise bigger than they were keeping.
  • They wrote it in public, where it could be quoted back at them.

What we know

✅ They wrote, in public, that a government’s power comes from the people — and that people have rights no king gave them. That claim is in the Declaration, dated July 4, 1776.

❌ They did not include everyone in that “people.” Enslaved people, women, and Native nations were left out, and the people left out said so in writing at the time.

The takeaway: The Declaration is a promise on paper, not a finished country. Read the sentence. Then read who was already using it. Next comes the Constitution — the rules they built, including the bargains they made.
