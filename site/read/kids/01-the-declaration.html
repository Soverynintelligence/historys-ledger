The story

Read the paper, then the people left out.

The promise

They wrote it in public: a government’s power comes from the people. People have rights no king gave them. That promise is in the Declaration, dated July 4, 1776.

Who was left out

They did not include everyone in that “people.” Enslaved people, women, and Native nations were left out. The people left out said so in writing at the time.

Both sides come from the same papers. We did not make this up.

They put it on paper

In 1776, most countries had a king. Kings said they were in charge because they were born that way, or because God picked them.

The people who left Britain wrote a new reason. They wrote it where anyone could read it.

A government only gets its power if the people agree. If it stops protecting their rights, the people can change it.

They signed that paper. They sent it into the world. That paper is the Declaration of Independence. The date on it is July 4, 1776.

Self-evident means they thought you should not need a king to tell you this. Equal here means born with the same rights. It does not mean every person is the same height, or the same rich.

The next part of the same sentence names three of those rights: life, liberty, and the pursuit of happiness. It says governments are built to protect those rights. Their power comes from the people.

Why they were angry

For years Britain had been taxing the colonies. The fight was not only that the taxes were high. The colonies had no vote in the group that taxed them. That group was called Parliament.

A writer named Thomas Paine said the setup was backwards. He used words ordinary people could read:

He meant: a huge place should not be run by a small island far away. That paper spread fast. Then came the Declaration.

The people left out said so right away

Here is the part many stories skip. Nobody had to wait a hundred years to see the problem. The people who were left out used the writers’ own sentence against them — at the time.

When they wrote “” hundreds of thousands of people in the new country were enslaved. Women could not vote. Native nations were treated as being in the way.

Abigail Adams wrote to her husband John while he was helping make the new laws:

She asked him to remember women. His answer was to laugh.

Enslaved people used the Declaration too. In Massachusetts, a woman named Elizabeth Freeman heard a constitution read aloud that said all men are born free and equal. She walked to a lawyer, sued, and won her freedom in court.

The words were already doing work the writers had not fully meant. Other people could pick the sentence up. That is why it mattered.

What the Declaration is — and is not

The Declaration is a promise on paper. It says what they thought a government is for. It is not the rulebook for how the country would run each day. That rulebook comes next: the Constitution, in 1787.

You can hold two true things at once:

  • They wrote a promise bigger than they were keeping.
  • They wrote it in public, where it could be quoted back at them.

What we know

✅ They wrote it in public: a government’s power comes from the people. People have rights no king gave them. That promise is in the Declaration, dated July 4, 1776.

❌ They did not include everyone in that “people.” Enslaved people, women, and Native nations were left out. The people left out said so in writing at the time.

The takeaway: The Declaration is a promise on paper, not a finished country. Read the sentence. Then read who was already using it. Next comes the Constitution — the rules they built, and the deals they made.
