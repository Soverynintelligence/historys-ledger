# How Europe walked in
**Modern Wars · 1914**  ·  *Thread: Total War & Its Costs*

> *"it is clear that the peace of Europe cannot be preserved."*
> — **Sir Edward Grey**, House of Commons, 3 August 1914

## The Clock

The war did not arrive as a surprise. It arrived as paperwork with a deadline.

Vienna dated the instruction on 22 July 1914 and ordered it handed in Belgrade the afternoon of the 23rd. The note said the Sarajevo murder had been conceived at Belgrade. It demanded, among ten points, that Austro-Hungarian organs take part in a judicial inquiry on Serbian soil. And it set the clock:

> *"The Imperial and Royal Government awaits the reply of the Royal Government by Saturday, the twenty-fifth instant, at 6 p.m., at the latest."*
> — **Austro-Hungarian ultimatum to Serbia**, 23 July 1914

**Primary Source:** Austro-Hungarian ultimatum to Serbia (English translation), BYU World War I Document Archive.

The same note named the demand that would not fit inside a constitution:

> *"6. to institute a judicial inquiry against every participant in the conspiracy of the twenty-eighth of June who may be found in Serbian territory; the organs of the Imperial and Royal Government delegated for this purpose will take part in the proceedings held for this purpose;"*
> — **Austro-Hungarian ultimatum**, clause 6

That is not a request for condolences. It is a foreign police power, written down, with forty-eight hours on it.

## What Belgrade Accepted, and What It Would Not

Serbia answered on the 25th. It did not refuse everything. On the societies Vienna blamed, Belgrade folded:

> *"Notwithstanding this, the Royal Government will accept the demand of the I. and R. Government and dissolve the society Narodna Odbrana, as well as every society which should set against Austria-Hungary."*
> — **Serbian reply**, 25 July 1914, clause 2

**Primary Source:** Serbian response to the Austro-Hungarian ultimatum (English translation).

On the foreign inquiry, it would not:

> *"As far as the cooperation in this investigation of specially delegated officials of the I. and R. Government is concerned, this cannot be accepted, as this is a violation of the constitution and of criminal procedure."*
> — **Serbian reply**, clause 6

The reply offered The Hague, or the Great Powers, if Vienna was unsatisfied. Vienna was unsatisfied. On 28 July Berchtold telegraphed that Austria-Hungary "considers herself henceforward in state of war with Serbia."

**Primary Source:** Austro-Hungarian declaration of war on Serbia, 28 July 1914.

## The Declarations That Followed

On 1 August, at St. Petersburg, the German ambassador handed Sazonov a note. After charging that Russian mobilisation had not waited, it closed:

> *"His Majesty the Emperor, my august Sovereign, in the name of the German Empire, accepts the challenge, and considers himself at war with Russia."*
> — **German declaration of war on Russia**, 1 August 1914

**Primary Source:** German declaration of war on Russia.

On 3 August, at 6.45 p.m. in Paris, Schoen handed Viviani a farewell letter. It listed alleged French flights over Belgium and bombs near German railways, then:

> *"in the presence of these acts of aggression the German Empire considers itself in a state of war with France in Consequence of the acts of this latter Power."*
> — **German declaration of war on France**, 3 August 1914 (Yellow Book No. 147)

**Primary Source:** French Yellow Book No. 147. Viviani, in the next paper, formally challenged the allegations. The ledger holds both sentences. It does not pick a winner between them.

## Passage, or the Decision of Arms

Belgium had a treaty of neutrality, guaranteed by the Powers, including Prussia. On 2 August, at 7 p.m. in Brussels, Germany asked for "friendly neutrality" that meant German troops on Belgian roads, and named the other door:

> *"Should Belgium oppose the German troops, and in particular should she throw difficulties in the way of their march by a resistance of the fortresses on the Meuse, or by destroying railways, roads, tunnels, or other similar works, Germany will, to her regret, be compelled to consider Belgium as an enemy."*
> — **German note to Belgium**, 2 August 1914 (Grey Book No. 20)

And:

> *"In this event, Germany can undertake no obligations towards Belgium"*
> — **same note**, fourth declaration

The held text then leaves the rest to the decision of arms. A twelve-hour limit was allowed. Belgium answered at 7 a.m. on 3 August:

> *"The Belgian Government, if they were to accept the proposals submitted to them, would sacrifice the honour of the nation and betray their duty towards Europe."*
> — **Belgian reply**, 3 August 1914 (Grey Book No. 22)

**Primary Source:** Belgian Grey Book, Nos. 20 and 22.

That afternoon Grey told the Commons the peace of Europe was gone, and that running from the Belgian treaty would leave Britain without the respect any leftover force would need. He also priced the war for people who thought standing aside was safety:

> *"We are going to suffer, I am afraid, terribly in this war whether we are in it or whether we stand aside."*
> — **Sir Edward Grey**, 3 August 1914

**Primary Source:** Hansard, House of Commons, 3 August 1914.

On the morning of 4 August the Belgian General Staff reported the territory violated at Gemmenich.

## The Chancellor Names the Wrong

The same day, in Berlin, the Imperial Chancellor told the Reichstag what the crossing was. The Belgian minister in Berlin reported the words:

> *"We are in a state of legitimate defence, and necessity knows no law."*
> — **Theobald von Bethmann Hollweg**, 4 August 1914, as reported in Grey Book No. 35

And:

> *"This is contrary to the dictates of international law. … For the wrong which we are thus doing, we will make reparation as soon as our military object is attained."*
> — **same speech**

**Primary Source:** Belgian Grey Book No. 35. The minister added his own sentence on the same page: the Chancellor recognised, without disguise, that Germany was violating international law.

These papers do not give the dead. They give the bargain: a clock, a refused inquiry, a road through a guaranteed country, and a named wrong paid later — after the object was attained.

## What We Learned

**The honest ledger:**

✅ **The walk is in the documents, not in a mood.** Ultimatum, reply, war telegram, two German declarations, a Belgian refusal, and a British speech to the Commons — dated, signed, and still readable. Europe did not "stumble." Cabinets wrote deadlines and then met them.

❌ **The cost is written in the same ink as the march.** Belgium was offered independence after the war if it opened the roads, and "the decision of arms" if it did not. Grey said the suffering would come whether Britain entered or stood aside. The German Chancellor called the occupation of Belgium contrary to international law in the same speech that asked the Reichstag to accept it. Necessity was the argument. The treaty was the thing it rode over.

**The takeaway:** 1914 is the setup so 1939 is not magic. The method is already here: notes with clocks, a small country's neutrality treated as a road, and a public admission that the law was being broken for a military object. The next chapter is the bill after twenty more years of that method. Here are the papers. You decide what "walked in" means.
