# Victory and the Bill — American papers, not the war
**Modern Wars · not a complete WWII set**

This folio holds ten quotations from seven papers we hold. It is not a history of the Second World War. It does not hold the fighting in Europe or the Pacific. It does not hold papers on the Holocaust. What it holds is American entry, Executive Order 9066 and *Korematsu*, the German and Japanese surrender instruments, and the President's announcement of the atomic bomb at Hiroshima.

> *"Yesterday, December 7, 1941—a date which will live in infamy—the United States of America was suddenly and deliberately attacked by naval and air forces of the Empire of Japan."*
> — **Franklin D. Roosevelt**, Address to Congress, December 8, 1941

## The entry these papers hold

Roosevelt's address of December 8, 1941 is the paper we hold for American entry. He told Congress that the day before, December 7, the United States had been attacked by naval and air forces of the Empire of Japan. The sentence that survives in memory is the one he actually spoke:

> *"a date which will live in infamy"*
> — **FDR**, December 8, 1941

**Primary Source:** FDR address to Congress, December 8, 1941.

He asked Congress to declare that a state of war had existed with the Japanese Empire since that attack. These papers do not hold a German or Italian declaration, and they do not hold the fighting that followed.

## What the Alliance said it was for

Before that request, Roosevelt and Churchill had already put aims on paper. The **Atlantic Charter** of August 14, 1941 disclaimed conquest:

> *"First, their countries seek no aggrandizement, territorial or other"*
> — **Atlantic Charter**, August 14, 1941

And further:

> *"they respect the right of all peoples to choose the form of government under which they will live"*
> — **Atlantic Charter**, August 14, 1941

**Primary Source:** Atlantic Charter, August 14, 1941.

That language is in the paper. It is a stated aim, and later conduct can be read against it. This folio does not hold the occupations, colonial settlements, or postwar governments that would be measured by it.

## 9066 and Korematsu

The legal instrument we hold is **Executive Order 9066** (February 19, 1942). It did not name Japanese Americans. It authorized military commanders to designate zones:

> *"any or all persons may be excluded"*
> — **Executive Order 9066**, February 19, 1942

**Primary Source:** Executive Order 9066, February 19, 1942.

In **1944**, the Supreme Court upheld Fred Korematsu's conviction for remaining in a restricted area. Justice Black's majority opinion, defending the wartime frame, had already written the rule that should have stopped it:

> *"all legal restrictions which curtail the civil rights of a single racial group are immediately suspect"*
> — **Korematsu v. United States**, 1944

And:

> *"racial antagonism never can"*
> — **Korematsu v. United States**, 1944 (on when such restrictions may stand)

**Primary Source:** Korematsu v. United States, 1944.

These two papers are the home-front record this folio holds. They are not a complete account of incarceration, and they are not a substitute for it.

## The surrenders these papers hold

The Allied declaration of June 5, 1945 stated the German result without euphemism:

> *"The German armed forces on land, at sea and in the air have been completely defeated and have surrendered unconditionally"*
> — **Allied Declaration on the Defeat of Germany**, June 5, 1945

**Primary Source:** German Instrument of Surrender, May 1945.

That is what this paper records: defeat and unconditional surrender. It does not record the fighting that produced that result, and it does not record the Holocaust.

Japan's formal surrender document of September 2, 1945 opened with unconditional language:

> *"We hereby proclaim the unconditional surrender to the Allied Powers of the Japanese Imperial General Headquarters and of all Japanese armed forces and all armed forces under the Japanese control wherever situated."*
> — **Japanese Instrument of Surrender**, September 2, 1945

**Primary Source:** Japanese Instrument of Surrender, September 2, 1945.

## The bomb these papers hold

In the paper we hold, President Truman announced the use of an atomic bomb at Hiroshima:

> *"It is an atomic bomb. It is a harnessing of the basic power of the universe."*
> — **Harry S. Truman**, August 6, 1945

**Primary Source:** Truman statement on Hiroshima, August 6, 1945.

These papers establish that the bomb was announced as used on Hiroshima, and that Japan later signed unconditional surrender. They do not settle whether the bomb was necessary. They do not hold the fighting in the Pacific, and they do not stand in for it.

## What We Learned

**The honest ledger:**

✅ **These papers record a request for war, stated aims, and two unconditional surrenders.** Roosevelt asked Congress to recognize a state of war after the December 7 attack. The Atlantic Charter disclaimed conquest and named the right of peoples to choose their government. The German and Japanese instruments record unconditional surrender. Truman announced the use of an atomic bomb at Hiroshima.

❌ **The same set records exclusion at home and a new weapon used on a city.** Executive Order 9066 authorized commanders to exclude "any or all persons." *Korematsu* sustained a conviction under that wartime frame, while writing that racial restrictions are immediately suspect and that racial antagonism never can justify them. The bomb announcement is in Truman's own words.

**The takeaway:** This folio is not a World War II set. It does not cover the fighting or the Holocaust. It holds ten quotations from seven papers: American entry, 9066 and *Korematsu*, the German and Japanese surrenders, and the bomb. The documents establish what was ordered, what was said, and what was signed. They do not stand in for the war.
