# The Bullet and the Podium — What We Ask of a Man Who Has Just Been Shot
**Chapter 7 · 1865–2024**  ·  *Thread: Violence & the Presidency*

> *"I don't know whether you fully understand that I have just been shot; but it takes more than that to kill a bull moose."*
> — **Theodore Roosevelt**, Milwaukee Auditorium, 14 October 1912

## The Light

Start with the part that is simply, unarguably true: a man was shot in the chest and gave a speech anyway.

On 14 October 1912, Theodore Roosevelt was leaving the Gilpatrick Hotel in Milwaukee, on his way to a campaign address, when **John Flammang Schrank** shot him at close range. The bullet passed through a steel eyeglass case and a fifty-page speech folded double in his breast pocket — *Progressive Cause Greater Than Any Individual* — and lodged in his chest.

What he did next is the reason we still tell it. Roosevelt was an experienced hunter and a competent anatomist. He reasoned that because he was not coughing blood, the bullet had not reached his lung. He was right. X-rays later confirmed it had stopped in the chest muscle without penetrating the pleura. He made that call standing in the street, bleeding, with minutes to decide.

He went to the auditorium and spoke. He pulled the manuscript from his coat and held it up — two holes through every page.

**Primary Source:** Speech manuscript, *Progressive Cause Greater Than Any Individual*, with bullet perforation; steel spectacle case, 1912.

Surgeons decided removal was more dangerous than leaving it. He carried the bullet until he died.

> *"I do not mind it any more than if it were in my waistcoat pocket."*
> — **Theodore Roosevelt**, on the bullet he kept

That is not a myth. It happened. Hold onto it, because the rest of this chapter is about what we build on top of it.

## The Number That Isn't There

The version of this story that circulates online says Roosevelt spoke for **84 minutes**. It is a good number — specific, confident, the kind of detail that sounds checked.

It is not established. Accounts give fifty minutes, eighty-four, ninety, "an hour-plus," and "nearly an hour and a half." They cannot all be right, and no contemporary record settles it.

What is defensible is this: **he spoke for over an hour with a bullet in his chest.** That is astonishing, and it is true, and it needs no help.

The precise figure is what a story grows when it is retold rather than checked. Note the mechanism, because it operates on everything that follows: a memorable event accumulates specificity it never had, and the specificity is what makes people believe the rest.

## Who Does Not Get a Story

Roosevelt lived. That is the entire reason we have his line.

**Abraham Lincoln** was shot at Ford's Theatre on 14 April 1865 and died the following morning without regaining consciousness. **John F. Kennedy** was shot in Dallas on 22 November 1963 and was declared dead within the hour. Neither man said anything memorable afterward, because neither man had an afterward.

This is the flaw in reading a pattern out of these events. The ones we remember for their composure are, necessarily, the ones who survived to display it. We are not observing a trait that distinguishes great men. We are observing the ones the bullet missed by enough.

That does not diminish Roosevelt or anyone else who stood up. It means the standing up is not the lesson we usually draw from it.

## The Myth Built in Real Time

On 30 March 1981, **John W. Hinckley, Jr.** shot **Ronald Reagan** outside the Washington Hilton. A bullet ricocheted off the limousine and entered under his left arm.

The public got the jokes, and they were real. To Nancy Reagan: *"Honey, I forgot to duck."* To the surgical team: *"I hope you're all Republicans."* Within a decade that composure had helped make him, in one historian's phrase, politically untouchable.

Here is what the public did not get. The bullet stopped roughly **an inch from his heart**. His lung collapsed. He bled internally, losing somewhere between forty percent and half of the blood in his body — about 3.7 quarts — before surgery. He was seventy years old. The agent who redirected the motorcade to the hospital rather than the White House was later blunt about the margin:

> *"Without Giordano and the doctors at GW, Ronald Reagan would have died."*
> — **Jerry Parr**, Secret Service

**Primary Source:** FBI investigative file, attempted assassination of President Ronald Reagan, 1981.

And while that was true, the hospital told the press he *"was never in any serious danger."*

That is the chapter in one sentence. The courage was genuine **and** the invulnerability was manufactured, at the time, by people managing a story. Both things happened at once. A history that reports only the jokes is repeating a press line, not a fact.

## Butler

On 13 July 2024, at a rally in Butler, Pennsylvania, **Thomas Matthew Crooks**, twenty years old, fired from a rooftop outside the venue. A bullet grazed **Donald Trump's** ear. Secret Service agents took him to the ground; he rose with his fist raised, and the photograph was on front pages worldwide within hours. The FBI later concluded Crooks acted alone.

**Primary Source:** FBI statement and investigative updates on the incident at Butler, Pennsylvania, 2024.

It is too recent for us to know what it means. It is not too recent to record who else was there.

**Corey Comperatore**, fifty years old, a former fire chief, was killed in the same volley. **David Dutch** and **James Copenhaver** were shot and survived.

The image that travelled was the raised fist. The man who died shielding his family did not travel with it. That is not a criticism of anyone who stood up — it is an observation about what a photograph selects, and what it leaves out of frame.

## What This Chapter Is Actually About

There is a real thing here, and it is worth naming plainly: under sudden mortal threat, several men in this record did not panic. Roosevelt diagnosed himself correctly in the street. Reagan made a room of surgeons laugh. That is not nothing. It is uncommon, and it is admirable, and anyone claiming otherwise is arguing with the record.

But three things sit alongside it, and a ledger has to carry all four:

1. **We only ever see the survivors.** Lincoln and Kennedy were not less resilient. They were shot better.
2. **The story gets embellished in transit.** Roosevelt's eighty-four minutes are an invention of retelling, and the invention is what makes the story feel verified.
3. **The story gets managed on purpose.** Reagan's "never in any serious danger" was issued while he was closest to dying.

The resilience is real. So is the machinery that turns it into something larger than it was. You can admire the first without accepting the second, and the whole point of reading from sources is that you do not have to choose between them on someone else's word.
